Abstract
We construct a kinematic phase space for E-mini S&P 500 futures by plotting
price momentum against its rate of change, revealing a dissipative spiral attractor
that persists from 1-minute to weekly timescales and across six major futures markets.
Applying sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy) to 15 years of minute-level
data, the algorithm discovers that momentum dynamics follow a stochastic damped
harmonic oscillator (SDHO), a structure that emerges from the data rather than by a
priori assumption.
Three dimensionless quantities characterize these dynamics: dissipation rate Ω≈
1.15, deterministic fraction R2 ≈0.57, and scaled restoring force Φ (market-specific).
Cross-market analysis reveals that Ωand R2 are universal across asset classes (CV
< 4%), while Φ varies by more than an order of magnitude, encoding asset-specific
information about mean-reversion intensity. The Fisher information metric on the
SDHO parameter space is diagonal in (k, Ω, σ), which provides a geometric explanation
for the observed universality: Ωis isolated on the statistical manifold, and Φ = σ2/(2Ω)
absorbs the coordinate dependence of k.
Eigenvalue analysis places the system near the critical damping boundary, with
characteristic decay times of ≈1-4 hours at hourly aggregation and a half-life of ≈2.5
hours for momentum shocks. The look-back parameter controls the transition between
underdamped and overdamped regimes, a finding with natural economic content: the
near-critical equilibrium may reflect a dynamical balance between mean-reversion and
trend-following strategies. We connect these results to Liouville’s theorem, contact
geometry for dissipative systems, and the Hurst exponent, providing a mechanistic
explanation for the widely documented anti-persistence (H < 0.5) at short horizons.
At weekly and longer horizons, the linear SDHO gives way to nonlinear dynamics,
developed in a companion paper (Dean, 2026).
Notes: We use “stable” in the empirical sense of consistency across timescales, markets,
and coordinate choices, not as a claim of mathematical invariance. The reported R2 reflects
conditional variance of momentum acceleration, not return predictability or tradable alpha.

1. Introduction
1.1 Motivation
Financial markets combine predictable structure with apparent randomness. While the
efficient market hypothesis suggests price changes should be unpredictable, empirical evidence
reveals patterns: momentum effects at intermediate horizons, mean reversion at short horizons,
and volatility clustering across timescales. This raises a question: can we identify stable
parameters that characterize market dynamics?
The answer is yes, but what may be of additional interest is the representation of market
dynamics within the broader context of dynamical systems information geometry, where we
see many beautiful structures emerging that have economic significance.
This paper applies such an approach to financial markets, but rather than analyzing price
returns directly, we construct a phase space representation from actual market data, plotting
market momentum against its rate of change, and then analyze the resulting flow field. This
approach has been considered in other application areas (Dean, 1999), and motivates the
application here. Indeed, as we discuss in the paper, the market phase diagrams display
a non-trivial spiral topology, typical for a dissipative dynamical system, a fact that is not
immediately obvious from a standard time series analysis of market price action.
This paper takes an empirical-first approach. Rather than postulating a dynamical model
and then fitting parameters, we construct a kinematic phase space from price data, examine
the topological structure of the resulting flow field, and apply sparse identification methods
to derive the governing equations. And as mentioned, the dynamical system that emerges is
exactly the stochastic damped harmonic oscillator (SDHO), a structure discovered by the
SINDy algorithm rather than assumed, as is often the case in the standard approach to
analyzing the market (Bouchaud and Cont, 1998; Sandoval and De Paula Franca, 2011).
1.2 Market as a Dynamical System
To analyze the market as a dynamical system, consider a simple construction: plot price
momentum on one axis and its rate of change on the other. The momentum coordinate x
measures recent trend (a 5-bar log return), while the acceleration coordinate y = ∆x (i.e.,
change in momentum) measures whether that trend is strengthening or weakening. Every
moment in time becomes a point in this two-dimensional phase space; the market’s evolution
2


traces a trajectory through it. In fact, each point in this diagram represents an initial
condition of the market state, and so what we see is the dynamical evolution for every initial
condition, displaying the totality of what is possible from a dynamical systems perspective.
Figure 1 shows the structure that emerges from 15 years of 1-min E-mini S&P 500 futures
data, aggregated to 1 hour candles, showing a distinct spiral structure, with trajectories
curving inward toward the origin. This is not how random walks appear: random walks
produce diffuse, directionless flow fields. What appears here is the signature of a dissipative
dynamical system, the same topology that governs a damped harmonic oscillator. Eigenvalue
analysis in Section 6 reveals that the system sits near the critical damping boundary, where
the interplay of deterministic dynamics and stochastic forcing produces the spiral structure
visible in the phase portraits. We make the point that the market evolution depends on both
deterministic and stochastic components, and it is the deterministic component that is shown
in Figure 1, as we will discuss in more detail below.
Natural questions follow: Is this structure robust across timescales? Does it appear in other
markets? Can we be systematic about developing the equations that govern it? This paper
addresses each question.
1.3 Main Contributions
The paper makes contributions in four areas: on the empirical side, the phase portraits reveal
that futures price momentum exhibits a stable dissipative attractor with spiral topology,
persisting from 1-minute to weekly timescales and across multiple asset classes. The system
sits near the critical damping boundary, where the look-back parameter controls the transition
between underdamped and overdamped regimes, a finding that turns out to have natural
economic content, as discussed in Section 6.1.
Next, we apply sparse identification (SINDy) to futures data in a phase space setting, letting
the data dictate the governing equations from a library of candidate functions. This inverts
the standard approach in which the dynamical equations are postulated and then fit to data;
here the functional form itself is discovered.
The quantitative contribution is the identification of three dimensionless parameters, dissipa-
tion rate Ω, deterministic fraction R2, and scaled restoring force Φ, that remain stable across
five orders of magnitude in timescale and characterize the balance between deterministic
and stochastic dynamics. The first two are universal across asset classes; the third encodes
market-specific information about mean-reversion intensity.
Finally, on the theoretical side, we connect these empirical findings to standard frameworks
from dynamical systems theory, including Liouville’s theorem for phase space contraction,
information geometry for coordinate-independent characterization, and contact geometry for
the proper treatment of dissipative systems.

1.4 Related Literature
Our approach differs from earlier phase space applications in finance. Recurrence plot analysis
(Bastos and Caiado, 2011; Marwan et al., 2007) use Takens embedding (Takens, 1981) to
reconstruct an abstract phase space; this is useful for detecting regime changes but the
reconstructed dimensions lack direct physical interpretation. In contrast, we construct a
kinematic phase space where coordinates have explicit economic meaning.
4


The damped oscillator model has appeared in finance before (Stádník, 2014). Bouchaud and
Cont (1998) developed a Langevin approach to market fluctuations, and Sandoval and De
Paula Franca (2011) used damped oscillators to model price recovery after shocks. However,
these papers simply assume the oscillator structure, and then fit parameters. Our contribution
is that SINDy discovers this structure from a candidate library containing nonlinear terms
up to a given order.
Safari and Schmidhuber (2025) recently documented timescale-dependent transitions between
trending and reverting regimes across multiple asset classes, finding that markets exhibit
mean-reversion at short horizons and momentum at longer horizons.
Our phase space
framework provides a geometric visualization of this phenomenon and identifies the governing
equations. Their empirical findings align closely with what we observe: the spiral attractor
structure we document corresponds to the mean-reversion regime they identify at shorter
timescales.
The SINDy algorithm was developed by Brunton, Proctor, and Kutz (2016), with extensions
to stochastic systems by Boninsegna, Nüske, and Clementi (2018). It has been widely used
in physics and engineering but, to our knowledge, not previously to financial data in this
kinematic framework.

2.2 Phase Space Construction
The idea behind phase space construction (Dean, 1999) is borrowed from classical mechanics,
where the state of a system is described not just by its position but also by its velocity. Here
we do the same thing with price data: rather than looking at momentum alone, we plot
momentum alongside its rate of change, giving us a two-dimensional picture of the market’s
state at every moment in time.
Concretely, the momentum coordinate is a log return over a look-back window:

Together, the pair (xt, yt) locates the market in phase space at time t. The interpretation is
intuitive: x tells us which direction the market has been moving (and how much), while y
tells us whether that move is gaining or losing steam.
To construct the flow field shown in Figure 1, we discretize this phase space into a grid and
compute the average next-period displacement from each cell. The result is a vector field that
reveals the expected direction and magnitude of motion from any starting point, essentially a
map of where the market “wants to go” from every possible initial condition.
2.3 Scale Invariance
Is the spiral structure of Figure 1 an artifact of aggregation timescale? To test this, we
aggregated minute-level data to multiple timescales: 1 minute, 5 minutes, 15 minutes, 1 hour,
4 hours, and daily. The results are shown in Figure 2 where we see that the persistence across
timescales argues against a pure microstructure explanation. Bid-ask bounce dominates at
high frequencies but averages out at hourly or daily aggregation. But the central spiral
structure persists across five orders of magnitude in observation count.

2.5 Universal vs. Market-Specific Features
The variation in Φ maps to known asset characteristics. Energy markets (CL) have storage
constraints and physical delivery that create strong mean-reversion (high Φ). Currency
markets (6E) follow near-random-walk dynamics due to uncovered interest parity (low Φ).
The universality of Ωand R2 suggests these quantities reflect structural properties of liquid
market microstructure rather than asset-specific dynamics.
These results paint a clear picture of what is universal and what is not.
The dissipation rate Ω= 1.15 ± 0.04 is remarkably consistent across all six markets, from

equity indices to Treasury bonds to currency pairs. This suggests that the rate at which
momentum shocks dissipate reflects a universal property of liquid markets, one likely rooted
in order flow exhaustion, market maker inventory management, and arbitrage activity, rather
than anything specific to the underlying asset.
The consistency is worth emphasizing:
momentum perturbations decay at essentially the same rate whether one is trading crude oil
or the euro.
The deterministic fraction R2 = 0.57 ± 0.01 is equally stable. The balance between predictable
mean-reversion and unpredictable noise appears to be a structural constant of liquid futures
markets, independent of asset class. Roughly 57% of momentum dynamics are systematic,
everywhere we look.
The scaled restoring force Φ, by contrast, varies by more than an order of magnitude, from
0.016 in currencies to 0.292 in energy, and this variation turns out to encode fundamental
information about how different asset classes anchor to equilibrium. Table 3 organizes these

3. Economic Interpretation of the Topology
3.1 The Two Economic Forces
The spiral topology is not just a mathematical curiosity, it has a direct economic reading.
The SDHO dynamics decompose into two forces, each with a clear market interpretation.
The restoring force (−kx) pushes acceleration in the opposite direction to momentum. When
prices are elevated (positive momentum, right side of phase space), the restoring force
pushes acceleration negative; arbitrageurs sell the rally. When prices are depressed (negative
momentum), acceleration is pushed positive; contrarians buy the dip. This is the mean-
reversion engine, reflecting the collective action of value investors, statistical arbitrageurs,
and market makers managing inventory.
9


The damping force (−Ωy) causes acceleration itself to decay toward zero. Regardless of
position in phase space, acceleration naturally exhausts over time as order flow completes
and news gets priced in. This prevents perpetual oscillation and ensures markets settle rather
than cycling indefinitely.
The superposition of both forces creates the characteristic attractor visible in the phase
portraits. All trajectories are pulled toward equilibrium, but the path is curved rather than
direct, reflecting the oscillatory nature of price dynamics around fair value.
The mathematical structure generalizing to longer aggregation periods will be discussed in a
later paper (Dean, 2026).

4. Governing Equations and the SINDy Framework
4.1 The SINDy Framework
The phase portrait reveals what the dynamics look like: a stable dissipative attractor with
spiral topology. But what equations produce this structure? The visual evidence suggests a
damped oscillator, but rather than assume this form, we let the data decide.
Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) discovers governing equations from data.
Given observations of state variables and their derivatives, SINDy seeks sparse representations
in a library of candidate functions. We use a library containing polynomial terms up to third
order:
Θ(z) = [1, x, y, x2, xy, y2, x3, x2y, xy2, y3]
The algorithm fits coefficients for all terms, then applies sparsity-promoting regularization to
zero out terms that don’t contribute meaningfully. What remains is the simplest equation
consistent with the data.

yet retains enough stochastic character to make consistent exploitation difficult.
4.3 Interpretation and Domain of Validity
It is worth pausing to clarify what R2 ≈0.57 does and does not mean. The quantity we
are reporting is a conditional variance decomposition: it measures how much of the variance
in momentum acceleration is explained by the current phase space position (x, y). This
is not a claim about price predictability or tradable alpha. The dependent variable here
is ∆x (change in momentum), not returns, and the fit is in-sample rather than a forecast.
The distinction matters, because a high in-sample R2 on acceleration says nothing directly
about one’s ability to profit from the signal, it simply characterizes the balance between
deterministic and stochastic forces acting on momentum dynamics.
A natural follow-up question is: over what range of timescales does this linear SDHO
description hold? To find out, we tested the model across multiple aggregation levels, from
hourly to weekly, and the results are summarized in Table 5.
The picture that emerges is reassuring: R2 remains essentially unchanged from hourly through
daily timescales, and the slight uptick at weekly aggregation (0.591) is still consistent with
the linear model. The SDHO provides an effective description across this entire range.

5. Dimensionless Quantities
There is a subtlety lurking in the SINDy results that we have not yet addressed: coordinate
dependence. The fitted spring constant k changes dramatically with the momentum look-back
period: 2 bars gives k ≈0.65; 30 bars gives k ≈0.03. Same market, same data, factor of 20
difference. The reason is straightforward, a longer look-back stretches the momentum axis,
mechanically reducing k, but the consequence is important: k by itself is not a meaningful
characterization of the dynamics. To say anything robust, we need quantities that do not
depend on how we define the momentum coordinate.
By systematically varying both look-back period (2-30 bars) and aggregation timescale (1
minute to daily), we find three combinations that remain stable. The dissipation rate Ω≈
1.09 ± 0.05 (for look-backs ≥5) is naturally dimensionless. The deterministic fraction R2
≈0.57 ± 0.05 is a variance ratio, also dimensionless. And the scaled restoring force Φ =
k · Var(x) ≈0.046 ± 0.003 absorbs the coordinate dependence of k by multiplying it against
the variance of the coordinate it acts on, a construction whose deeper meaning will become
clear when we derive the Fisher metric in Section 6.2.

6.1 Liouville’s Theorem and Lyapunov Stability
With this dynamical model we can now connect the empirical findings to standard results
from dynamical systems theory. The first connection is to Liouville’s theorem, which says
that for a Hamiltonian system, phase space volume is conserved. Our system is manifestly
not Hamiltonian, and we can see this directly by computing the divergence of the flow field:

flow exhausts and prices equilibrate. The nonzero divergence is the geometric signature of
that dissipation.

character.
This raises an apparent puzzle: if the system is overdamped at the baseline look-back, why
do the phase portraits show such pronounced spiral structure? There are three reasons.
First, the system is close to critical. At n = 5, the restoring force sits at 73% of its critical
value (k/kcrit = 0.24/0.33 = 0.73), and this proximity means the slow and fast modes have a
substantial timescale ratio (τslow/τfast ≈3.3). An overdamped system near criticality does
not return to the origin along a straight line; its trajectories curve substantially as the fast
mode decays ahead of the slow mode.
Second, the stochastic component matters. With R2 ≈0.57, fully 43% of the dynamics
are noise-driven, and in a near-critically-damped system these random perturbations are
channeled along the curved trajectories, amplifying the spiral appearance through continual
excursions across phase space.
Third, the system genuinely transitions through the critical boundary as the measurement
timescale changes. At look-back n = 3, it is underdamped with true oscillatory decay. The
spiral topology visible in the phase portraits reflects this underlying oscillatory character at
the shortest timescales, which persists visually even as longer look-back parameters move
into the overdamped regime.
The economics here are suggestive. The slow eigenmode (τ ≈3.6 hours) sets the timescale on
which momentum perturbations dissipate, governed by arbitrage and order flow exhaustion.
The fast eigenmode (τ ≈1.1 hours) captures the rapid equilibration of acceleration, where
buying and selling pressure adjusts quickly. The half-life of the slow mode, t1/2 = ln 2/|λ1| ≈
16


2.5 hours, implies that momentum shocks lose half their intensity within a single trading
session, consistent with the intraday mean-reversion documented by Safari and Schmidhuber
(2025).
The proximity to critical damping may itself be economically meaningful. A market that is too
underdamped would oscillate excessively, creating opportunities for mean-reversion traders;
one that is too overdamped would settle too slowly, inviting trend followers. The near-critical
equilibrium may represent a dynamical balance between these competing strategies, consistent
with the adaptive markets hypothesis (Lo, 2004, 2017).
6.2 Information Geometry
In Section 5 we observed that Ω, R2, and Φ are stable across look-back periods while k
itself is not, and promised that the Fisher metric would explain why. Information geometry
(Amari, 2000) provides the framework: it views the parameter space of a statistical model as

the directions of maximum statistical distinguishability on the model manifold. The stability
we observe is not a coincidence, it is a consequence of the geometry of the parameter space.
6.3 Contact Geometry for Dissipative Systems
There is a natural question about geometric framework. Symplectic geometry is the standard
language for Hamiltonian systems (Arnold, 1989), and coordinate invariance in that setting
is well understood (Dean, 1993, 1996). But our system is dissipative, phase space volumes
shrink, as we saw from Liouville’s theorem, so symplectic structure is not preserved. What is
the right geometric framework?
The answer is contact geometry (Bravetti et al., 2017), which extends symplectic structure to
odd-dimensional manifolds by introducing an additional coordinate. In the present context, we
extend the two-dimensional phase space (x, y) to include an “entropy” or “action” coordinate
s, whose dynamics are:

6.4 Connection to the Hurst Exponent
A natural question is how the SDHO framework connects to the Hurst exponent, the standard
measure of long-range dependence in time series. Recall that H > 0.5 indicates persistence
(trending), H < 0.5 indicates anti-persistence (mean-reversion), and H = 0.5 corresponds to a
random walk.
Our framework provides a mechanistic explanation for the mean-reverting regime (H < 0.5)
that has been widely documented at short horizons. The attractor structure we observe,
with the restoring force (k > 0) pulling momentum back toward zero and damping (Ω>
0) causing acceleration to exhaust, is precisely the dynamical signature of anti-persistence.
When momentum mean-reverts, prices stabilize rather than trend, and this is exactly what
the SDHO predicts at intraday timescales.
The decay timescales we compute (τ ≈1-4 hours at hourly aggregation) align well with
independent empirical findings. Safari and Schmidhuber (2025) document mean-reversion at
short horizons transitioning to momentum (persistence) at longer horizons, and our framework
explains the short-horizon side of that transition: the restoring force represents value investors
and arbitrageurs pushing prices back toward fair value, while the damping represents order
flow exhaustion and news getting priced in.
The story gets more interesting at longer horizons where see that the transition to persistence
(H > 0.5) at monthly and longer timescales coincides with where our linear SDHO breaks
down. Preliminary analysis shows R2 increasing and nonlinear terms becoming significant,
suggesting a qualitatively different dynamical regime. The characterization of this persistence
regime, and its connection to the momentum anomaly of Jegadeesh and Titman (1993), is
developed in a companion paper (Dean, 2026).
6.5 Economic Interpretation
Each of the three invariants has a natural economic reading, and taken together they tell a
coherent story about how liquid markets process information.
The dissipation rate Ω≈1.15 measures how quickly buying or selling pressure exhausts
itself as orders are filled, market makers adjust quotes, and arbitrageurs trade against the
imbalance. A value greater than unity means this process is fast, most of an acceleration
shock is absorbed within a single bar, but not instantaneous. There is a brief window in
which the pressure is still live, and this is the window that the deterministic component of
the dynamics describes.
The deterministic fraction R2 ≈0.57 places the market near a boundary between order and
noise. If R2 were substantially higher, the predictable component would invite competition
19


from systematic traders, eroding the signal until predictability fell. If R2 were much lower, the
market would resemble a pure random walk, inconsistent with the documented microstructure
effects. The observed value may represent a quasi-stable equilibrium in which predictability is
limited enough to prevent easy exploitation but substantial enough to reward careful analysis,
consistent with the adaptive markets hypothesis (Lo, 2004, 2017).
Finally, the scaled restoring force Φ quantifies how aggressively prices revert to fair value, and
its cross-market variation (Table 2) maps onto known asset characteristics. Energy markets,
with storage constraints and physical delivery, exhibit the strongest mean reversion; currency
markets, governed by near-random-walk dynamics and uncovered interest parity, exhibit the
weakest. In this sense Φ is a fingerprint of the asset class, encoding the intensity of the
arbitrage mechanisms that anchor prices to fundamentals.
6.6 Limitations
Several caveats are worth noting. The primary analysis centers on a single market (ES),
though the cross-market analysis in Section 2 demonstrates that the key findings generalize
across asset classes. The linear SDHO model applies at intraday-through-weekly timescales;
at monthly and longer horizons, preliminary analysis indicates that nonlinear terms become
significant, and the dynamics appear qualitatively different.
We have treated the dimensionless quantities as static constants, but they may shift during
extreme market regimes. To test this, we estimated Ωand R2 on first-half versus second-
half subsamples and found values consistent within statistical uncertainty, which suggests
reasonable stability over the 15-year sample period, but a more granular rolling-window
analysis would be informative (see Section 6.7). Finally, the R2 ≈0.57 is an in-sample fit;
out-of-sample performance would almost certainly be lower, and as discussed in Section
4.3, this quantity characterizes the deterministic/stochastic decomposition of acceleration
dynamics, not return predictability.
6.7 Future Work
Several interesting directions follow from these results. The most immediate is the nonlinear
regime at longer horizons where preliminary analysis indicates that the linear SDHO approxi-
mation gives way to nonlinear dynamics and bifurcation theory at monthly timescales and
beyond. A companion paper (Dean, 2026) develops this analysis, including the connection to
the momentum anomaly literature.
A second direction is to estimate the dimensionless quantities in rolling windows, tracking
their evolution through time rather than treating them as static constants. VIX may serve as
a natural regime indicator here, distinguishing calm periods from crisis episodes and testing
whether Ωand R2 shift during market stress.
This connects to a third possibility: extending the phase space to include volatility (VIX)
as a third coordinate.
The current two-dimensional analysis captures momentum and
20


acceleration; adding a volatility axis could reveal richer attractor structure and connect the
SDHO framework to the variance risk premium literature.
Finally, the flow field representation provides a natural interface with modern machine
learning. The rich pattern structure visible in the phase plane is well-suited to generative
models that can sample trajectories respecting the discovered dynamics, enabling synthesis
of realistic market data. Reinforcement learning agents can exploit the low-dimensional state
space, with the dimensionless quantities serving as regime indicators. The invariance of Ω
and R2 across asset classes suggests transfer learning opportunities, where a model trained
on one market may generalize to others with only Φ needing recalibration.
7. Conclusion
Constructing a kinematic phase space from futures price momentum and applying sparse
identification methods, we discover that the dynamics at intraday-to-weekly timescales take
the form of a stochastic damped harmonic oscillator, a structure that arises from the data
rather than being imposed. The phase portraits display a dissipative spiral attractor that
persists from 1-minute to daily timescales and appears across all six markets tested.
Three dimensionless quantities characterize these dynamics. The dissipation rate Ω= 1.15
± 0.04 and the deterministic fraction R2 = 0.58 ± 0.01 are universal across asset classes
(CV < 4%), while the scaled restoring force Φ varies from 0.016 in currencies to 0.292 in
energy, encoding how aggressively each asset class mean-reverts. The invariance of Ωand
R2 suggests these quantities reflect fundamental properties of liquid market microstructure,
the rate at which order flow exhausts, the efficiency of arbitrage activity, and the balance
between predictable and unpredictable dynamics, that transcend specific asset classes. The
variation in Φ, by contrast, maps directly onto known differences in how prices anchor to
equilibrium: storage constraints and physical delivery in energy, uncovered interest parity in
currencies.
The Fisher metric on the SDHO parameter space turns out to be diagonal in (k, Ω, σ), and
this diagonality provides the geometric explanation for the observed stability: Ωis isolated
on the statistical manifold, and Φ = σ2/(2Ω) absorbs the coordinate dependence of k. The
system sits near the critical damping boundary, with the look-back parameter controlling
the transition between underdamped and overdamped regimes and characteristic momentum
decay times of 1-4 hours. The near-critical equilibrium may reflect a dynamical balance
between mean-reversion and trend-following strategies, consistent with the adaptive markets
hypothesis.
The finding that R2 ≈0.57 across all markets suggests that liquid futures operate at an
invariance boundary between deterministic and stochastic behavior, where predictability
is limited enough to prevent easy exploitation but substantial enough to reward careful
analysis. Whether these quantities are truly invariant in some deep sense, or merely stable
over the conditions we examined, remains to be seen. But the fact that they persist across
21


five orders of magnitude in timescale, across look-back definitions, and across asset classes
from equity indices to currencies is compelling evidence for genuine structural constants of
market dynamics.
At monthly and longer horizons, the linear SDHO gives way to nonlinear dynamics and
a qualitatively different regime. The characterization of this transition is developed in a
companion paper (Dean, 2026).
